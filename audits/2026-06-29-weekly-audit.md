## 3. The week's best post — and why

**The Advisor in Her Town (06-26).**

It worked, in Red Bull terms, because it gave a development director, on the second-to-last page of the post, a *new map of her own job.* Most of this blog's stance posts give the reader a sharper way of doing what she is already doing. The advisor post gives the reader a different *number* of people to do it with. The institution has been managing two relationships (donor and dev officer) when there are three (donor, dev officer, and the woman in green Sharpie on Pleasant Street). That is a perspective shift, not a tactic. It is the rare brand essay that, when forwarded to a colleague, requires the colleague to *change a screen in the CRM* before she can answer.

The three load-bearing pieces:

1. **Identity.** *"That is not the advisor failing the donor. That is the institution failing to brief its own translator."* The post lets the reader off the hook for the donor's behavior and puts her squarely on the hook for the advisor's. Identity work, made operational. A working dev director, by the end of that sentence, has either decided she is the kind of person who is going to write a colleague letter on Monday, or decided she is not.

2. **Adrenaline.** *"When the* Charitable Planning *tab opens in March and Arthur says* the college, *the advisor's eye will, before she says anything to her client, go to that letter. She will read the first line again."* The post has a clock in it. *In March.* Right now. Right *this March* — and the only thing in the room with Arthur's decision is the letter the institution did not send in 2014.

3. **Worldview.** This is the first post in the catalog to argue that the *structural* form of major-gift fundraising is wrong — not the tactics, not the calendar, the *form.* The job has the wrong number of people in it. That is a much harder thing to argue than "the wealth screen is wrong" or "the gala is the worst place to meet a donor." Those are tactical reframes; this is a structural one. *Red Bull does not sell sugar water. It sells a worldview in which jumping out of a balloon is a reasonable Tuesday.* This post sells a worldview in which the advisor is the reasonable Tuesday.

**Structural mirror for next week:** open on an object in someone else's office (not the donor's). Introduce the donor in paragraph two. Introduce the institution's failure in paragraph six. Skip the *Rōmy* graf entirely. Skip the assignment entirely. Close on a flat declarative under six words. When the post tries to give itself a closing aphorism *("The list is the will. The bench is the room. The shelf is the will."),* let it land on the floor instead. The post is doing too much work to need a chorus.

## 4. The week's worst post — and what it should have been

**She Moved in March (06-25).**

The thesis is correct. Eleanor Atwood's half-sheet on cream notepaper is, exactly as the post argues, the loudest unread signal in the file. The problem is that the prior audit said the same thing about 06-21 — and the writer responded by writing the same essay four days later with the dial turned up on the prop catalog (cream notepaper instead of bone china; Eleanor instead of Margaret; Brookhaven CCRC instead of "small museum"; daughter Anne in Brookline instead of daughter Helen in Bryn Mawr). This is the closet, again. The reframe is now exhausted: we have written the "address change is the signal" post three times in seven days (06-19, 06-21, 06-25). It is now the brand's signature interpretive move and it needs a 30-day rest.

What 06-25 should have been is the post the writer was three paragraphs from writing and walked away from. The most interesting character in *She Moved in March* is not Eleanor Atwood. It is **Theo, the new development associate, four months in, who has not met Mrs. Atwood, who opens the envelope on a Thursday at 9:14, ticks the *address verified by donor* box, drops the half-sheet in the records-to-shred bin, and goes back to the queue.** The whole transaction takes him four minutes. He does not call. Nobody calls.

The post the writer should have written is the post in which Theo, in late October, four months later, is in his apartment in Allston on a Tuesday evening, eating reheated lentil soup, with the institution's all-staff email on his phone showing that Eleanor Atwood passed away on the 19th of October, leaving the museum a bequest of nineteen million dollars — and he is sitting on his couch realizing he was the only person at the institution who had her new phone number for six months. The post is about *the development associate the institution did not invest in,* who had the new number on a half-sheet of cream notepaper and was given no reason to know what to do with it. The post is about the institution's failure to *train its junior staff to see signal in paper.* Theo is the entire pipeline of 2031, and the institution is paying him forty-six thousand a year to ignore him for fifteen months until he leaves for a job at a hedge fund's foundation.

**Suggested rewritten hook (2–3 lines):**

> Theo Marin is twenty-five years old. He has been the development associate at a small American art museum on the Charles River for one hundred and eleven days. The first envelope he opened on his first Thursday in February was a half-sheet of cream notepaper from a woman named Eleanor Atwood, who had given to the museum every year since 1979, and who had just moved into a continuing-care community in Lexington.
>
> Theo keyed in the new address at 9:14 a.m. He dropped the half-sheet in the records-to-shred bin. He did not call.
>
> The next time the institution finds out what was in that envelope is at a wake.

Same reframe. Different protagonist. Different city. Different generation. The institution looking, for once, at the person on its own payroll who is the entire shape of the next ten years.

## 5. Three angles to try next week — each a stance, not a topic

The prior audit's three angles produced one verbatim shipped post (#3, the advisor). The other two ("the daughter in Denver is the donor now" and "major giving has been redlined") are still on the table — explicitly. Do not retire them. Three additional ones:

1. **"The development associate is the major-gift officer."** Defendable: the under-25 staffer at the front of every shop is the one opening the envelopes on a Thursday at 9:14 a.m. The institution has spent zero hours teaching her how to read a half-sheet on cream notepaper. The next decade of revenue is being archived to a shred bin by a person we pay forty-six thousand dollars and lose to a hedge fund's foundation in eighteen months. This is the post 06-25 should have been. It also defends the brand's emerging stance that the institutional failure is structural, not tactical.

2. **"Major-gift portfolios are a fiction."** Defendable: the field standard says a major-gift officer "carries a portfolio" of 120 donors. The honest portfolio is fourteen. The other 106 are CRM rows in a quota slide. The post argues the profession should formally retire the 120-donor portfolio and adopt the dozen-donor caseload — and that the institution that does it first will, in eight years, be the institution that has the bequests. (Bonus: forces a sentence about staffing economics, a beat the blog has never touched.)

3. **"The naming opportunity is a receipt."** Defendable: the naming-opportunity arms race (bricks, benches, chairs, walls, foyers, third-floor reading rooms) is the field's worst self-inflicted commodity. The Rōmy stance: name fewer things. Name them after the donor's mother, her freshman roommate, the third-grade teacher she has not seen since 1962. The brick is a receipt. The dedication is the relationship. (This is also, conveniently, the post that gives the writer permission to never write about a brass plaque again, which she would benefit from.)

**Bar:** each of these should make the working fundraiser argue back at the headline. If she nods, we lost.
