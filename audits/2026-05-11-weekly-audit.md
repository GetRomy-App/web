## Patterns across the week

**1. The house style has consolidated, and it is the strongest writing this brand has shipped.** Five posts in a row in the same literary "field letter" register: named characters, sensory specificity (the cardinal in red ballpoint, the bowl burning her hand, the navy cardigan that had been to three of these galas), italicized interior dialogue, second-person address, intimate close. This is a real voice. Defend it.

**2. The product-demo intrusion is the only crack.** Posts 4 and 5 contain "open Romy / forty seconds later" scenes that name-check RōmyScore, "sixteen-section profile," "Liquidity & Tax-Planning category," "Solicitation Guardrails section." A literary frame cannot carry an ingredient list — the seams show. Compare to post 1, which earns the product mention with: *"The cardinal on the envelope. The nurse's name. The gift takes care of itself."* That is the entire product, named without naming. The other two posts should have done the same.

**3. "We've been thinking about [Name] a lot lately" appears verbatim in posts 1 and 2.** It is a beautiful line. It is now a tic. By post 3 it would be a brand catchphrase nobody wanted.

**4. "A small assignment, with love" closer appears in three of five posts** (1, 2, 3) and a numbered variant in post 4 ("The thirty-minute assignment"). Useful template, fast becoming formula. Vary the closer by mid-month or the reader's eye will skip it.

**5. Three of five posts open on a specific weekday-hour timestamp.** "3:14 on a Tuesday afternoon" / "10:47 on a Sunday night" / "Tuesday morning, coffee is still hot." Powerful device. Already a tell.

**6. Named characters land. Unnamed ones drift.** Carol, Mrs. Inouye, Beverly, Margaret Halloran, Anna — all stay with the reader. The "woman in the cardigan" and the "development director with nineteen tabs" — composite types — are forgettable. Pattern: when the post commits to a name, it works. When it hedges, it abstracts.

**7. "The boring revolution, again" is self-referencing twice in five days.** Post 2 and post 4 both invoke it. A brand catchphrase used twice in a week stops being a thread and starts being a tagline.

## Best post — "On Being Named in the Will"

**Why it worked, in Red Bull terms:** It sells an *identity*, not a feature. After reading it, a development director thinks "I want to be the person who knows what to say when Mrs. Inouye calls." That is the Red Bull move: the reader is now performing a worldview, not evaluating a tool.

Specifically:
- **The hook is sensory and stakes-loaded** in one sentence: "Carol picked up the phone at 3:14 on a Tuesday afternoon in March, which she remembers because she had just finished microwaving the second half of her lunch and the bowl was still hot in her hand." You cannot stop reading.
- **The stance is contrarian and defensible.** "Planned giving is not a program… The program is whether, when Mrs. Inouye calls on a Tuesday afternoon, the person who picks up the phone knows what to do with the next four minutes." Most fundraising blogs would never say a brochure is the wrong answer. This one does.
- **The product enters as a consequence, not a sales pitch.** "That is the file the tool should give you. Not a wealth score. Not a capacity rating. *The story.*" The reader arrives at the product themselves.
- **The close is operational, not aspirational.** "Find one donor in your file who has given a small amount, every year, for ten years or more." The reader has a thing to do on Monday morning.

Future posts should mirror this structure: scene → stance → consequence → assignment.

## Worst post — "Permission to Have an Evening"

Not bad. The weakest of the week because it is the most adulterated with feature-listing. The "different Sunday" middle is the offending paragraph:

> "You sit down at 9pm. You have a quiet hour and a cup of tea. You open Romy. You type the prospect's name. Forty seconds later, you have a sourced sixteen-section profile — RōmyScore 78 ('Very Strong'), foundation board service confirmed, $1.2M property in Hunts Point, three documented gifts to peer organizations in the $25K–$100K range, a recent liquidity event from a January business sale. There's a Solicitation Guardrails section telling you what *not* to bring up. There's a recommended ask range with the formula visible. Every claim is hyperlinked."

That is a product brochure inside a literary essay. The reader feels the genre switch and tunes out. What it should have been: one sentence. *"An hour later, you close the laptop. The salad is still in the fridge, uneaten, because you didn't need to eat at your desk."* Trust the reader to know what the product did in that hour.

The other weakness: the unnamed "development director" composite. Name her. Give her a city. The post would land twice as hard if she were Beth from Lansing.

**Rewritten hook (proposed):**

> At 10:47 on a Sunday night in March, somewhere in your city, a development director is alphabetizing dead men. Beth, in Lansing, has nineteen tabs open and half a salad gone cold. The ask is Tuesday. She has been doing this since the snow melted.
>
> We want to talk about Beth's Sunday nights.

That hook keeps the time-stamp, the salad, and the tabs (all working), adds a name and a city (raising it to "Mrs. Inouye" specificity), and lands on a stance verb — *alphabetizing dead men* — that does the whole essay's work in three words.

## Three angles to try next week

Each is a stance, not a topic. A defendable sentence the writer could put on a t-shirt.

**1. "The board's list is a lagging indicator."**
The major-gift program organized around board referrals is working from a stale signal. The development director's own coffee notes from 2019 outperform the chair's "you should call Helen" by a wide margin. Defend it. Pick a fight with the board-referral orthodoxy.

**2. "The wealth screen is a tax on attention."**
Reframe enterprise screening tools not as expensive in dollars but as expensive in *cognition*. Every five-figure license is silently teaching your team to look at property records when the signal was in your gift table the whole time. The line: "We didn't stop noticing donors because we got cheap tools. We stopped noticing donors because we got expensive ones."

**3. "The receipt is what costs you the bequest."**
Inverse of the thank-you piece. Take a specific, real-looking auto-receipt — header logo, IRS language, "Dear Valued Donor," return envelope tucked in — and walk through it sentence by sentence, naming what each line says to a 78-year-old donor who already kept your last receipt in a drawer. Show, by negative space, what every line of warmth costs in machined transactional language.

## Brand voice drift watch — phrases to retire or rest

Flag these to the daily writer. Each one was good once. Each one is overused now.

- **"We've been thinking about [Name] a lot lately."** Posts 1 and 2. Retire for at least four weeks.
- **"A small assignment, with love."** Three of five posts. Rotate. Try "A thing to try this week," "An exercise," or skip the section entirely on one post in three.
- **"The boring revolution, again."** Self-referenced in posts 2 and 4. Use only when the post genuinely earns it — once a month, max.
- **"In our heads / in our view / we'd like to help you read it / we're calling that off."** The soft brand-voice "we" is creeping in at the bottom of each post, exactly where the product enters. This is where the voice cracks every time. Either commit to a single-author "I" or speak as a confident "we" — but don't let the "we" go apologetic just because the URL is about to be mentioned.
- **The weekday-and-hour opener.** "3:14 on a Tuesday," "10:47 on a Sunday night," "Tuesday morning, coffee is still hot." Already a formula. Skip it in at least one post next week.
- **Italicized interior dialogue (*Carol said oh.*, *tell me about Robert.*).** Brilliant once. Pervasive in every post. Beautiful tic, but a tic. Use it once per post, not three times.
- **"With love" sign-offs.** Twice in a week is fine. Three weeks running is a Hallmark card.
