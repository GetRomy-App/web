## 3. The week's best post — and why

**The Desk by the Copier (07-05).**

It worked, in Red Bull terms, because it did what the prior audit named as the missing move of the catalog: **it stopped writing about the donor and started writing about the person who is in the room with the paper.** The blog has been, for six months, writing about widows in kitchens. This post is about *the twenty-four-year-old at the desk by the copier.* That is a category change — from *donor portrait* to *staff portrait* — and it is the category the brand needed.

The three load-bearing pieces:

1. **Identity.** *"Rowan is the front of your house. She is reading your next decade in ballpoint on notebook paper. Nobody has ever taught her what she is looking at."* The stance re-parameterizes the reader. Every previous Rōmy essay assumed the reader is Kip, the fifty-eight-year-old director of major gifts on the second floor. This post assumes she is *Kip's boss.* The identity work is *managerial:* it asks the reader to look one desk over from the person she has been managing, and see the person doing the highest-leverage work in her building. Red Bull identity work makes the reader want to *be* the kind of person who jumps. This post makes the reader want to be the kind of executive director who *promotes.* Different scale, same mechanism.

2. **Adrenaline.** *"The museum will, twenty-two months from this Thursday, learn what was in Cheryl Loveland's estate plan when the letter from a Wauwatosa attorney arrives naming the museum for one point two million dollars, memorialized in her mother's name."* The post has a clock in it, and the clock is running *right now.* The letter is in April. The attorney's letter comes in February of 2028. The reader is at the desk this Thursday. She is 22 months from the check. She can act. The stakes are legible. The horror is *ambient* — nothing has happened yet, everything is about to. That is the exact tension Red Bull films use: the guy at the top of the cliff has not jumped. Every frame is the *before.* This post is 22 months of *before.*

3. **Worldview.** The structural argument is that major-gift fundraising has the *wrong org chart.* The institution has been paying $141k to Kip on the second floor and $42k to Rowan by the copier — and Rowan is the one reading the actual pipeline. That is a structural claim, not a tactical one. It is the *staffing-economics* argument the prior audit flagged as the beat the blog had never touched. It has now been touched, sharply. A working ED will, on Monday, look at her staffing chart and see it differently. That is what a brand argument is for.

**Structural mirror for next week:** open on an action performed by the staff member the institution does not think about — the receptionist who intercepts the walk-in donor, the events coordinator who watches the room at the fifth-year reunion, the associate who runs the DAF reconciliations, the CFO who signs the annual audit report. Introduce the donor in paragraph four. Introduce the institution's failure in paragraph six. Skip the *"A small honest note about Rōmy"* header — put the Rōmy paragraph directly in the body, unbadged. Skip the assignment section entirely. Close on a two-sentence flat declarative under twelve words. This is now the third data point (06-26 *Advisor*, 07-05 *Desk*, and 06-27 *Reunion* half-form) that the *category-change* essay outperforms the *donor-portrait* essay by a full point of average score.

## 4. The week's worst post — and what it should have been

**The Girl on the Dock (07-02).**

The post concedes the pitch. The literal words are: *"Rōmy does not sit on the dock. The grandmother sits on the dock."* And then: *"That work is not, in the plainest sense, work a piece of software gets to do. It cannot be scheduled. It cannot be automated. It cannot be nudged by a well-timed drip campaign."* A marketing blog does not say those sentences. It is one thing to write a piece where the product plays a *supporting* role. It is another to write a piece that argues, structurally, that the product *has no role.* This post is Rōmy sponsoring an essay about how the grandmother is enough.

The frame is that a fourteen-year-old girl and her grandmother sit on a dock in New Hampshire and the grandmother tells the girl about a family that gave to a hospital in 1978. The girl will, in 2044, write a check to that hospital. The post's argument is that this transmission is what actually matters, and no CRM captures it. That is *true* — but it is not *Rōmy's story.* It is a story about the *limits* of any development-office intervention, being sponsored by the blog of a company selling development-office intervention.

The post the writer should have written is the *inversion* of this one: **the post is about the institution's failure to *notice the granddaughter.*** Not the grandmother. The granddaughter. Cora Bishop, 14, in July of 2026, is a person the little hospital in Berlin has never had in any file, on any list, in any address book. Cora is a *rising major-gift prospect* — thirty years out, but present at the dock this Sunday, receiving the transmission, and *findable in the public record* (an eighth-grade honor roll notice in a Concord paper, a lacrosse team photograph in a school newsletter, a dedication in her grandfather's memorial page from 2018).

The audit's prior stance holds here: the institution has been thinking of Ellen as the donor. Ellen is the *bridge.* Cora is the donor. The post the writer should have written is the post in which the little hospital in Berlin, in July of 2026, sends *Cora a first-name-only postcard* — because a development associate somewhere spent six minutes finding her name in the public record — thanking her, at fourteen, for a gift her family made in 1978, before she was born. The postcard goes in a small stack on the sideboard at the cottage. Cora reads it. Her grandmother reads it too. Ellen makes a five-figure gift in September. Cora becomes the seven-figure donor of 2044 not because of the dock conversation, but because *someone at the institution saw her, and said her name, at fourteen.*

That is the post. It is the *stewardship of the fourteen-year-old.* It is a beat the blog has never touched. It also, structurally, argues that Rōmy has a role — the role of *finding Cora in the public record* — because the manual work of surfacing a fourteen-year-old rising prospect from four small newspapers and a school newsletter is the work no development officer has ninety minutes for. That is a *pitch that lands.* The shipped post is a pitch that concedes.

**Suggested rewritten hook (2–3 lines):**

> Cora Bishop is fourteen. She lives most of the year in Newton, Massachusetts, and most Julys in a cottage on Squam Lake. ## 6. Brand voice drift watch — the new tics

### Standing rules — enforcement escalating

- **♡ glyph total for July: 11 in four posts.** The trend line from the ban's announcement is 4 → 6 → 11 — the ban is *inversely* correlated with the ship rate. The mechanism is now visible: the writer relocates the ♡ into whichever section the audit has *not* explicitly named. Body of post, quoted diegetic object, Sharpie inscription, section header interior. **New enforcement rule for the next two weeks:** the ♡ glyph is a *pre-flight rejection.* Any post that arrives with a ♡ character anywhere in the file — including inside a quoted object, inside a character's speech, inside a section header, inside a napkin inscription, inside a piece of Sharpie in someone's hand — is bounced back with no further review. The writer receives one sentence: *"Remove the eleven hearts and resubmit."* No editorial argument. No exceptions. No merit-based override. The brand is not its closing punctuation. This is the third audit in a row to say so.

- **"A small honest note about Rōmy" section header — 6 verbatim instances in 13 days.** The variant *"What Rōmy is doing about it"* (07-05) is now the seventh. **New rule:** the *Rōmy* paragraph ships *inside the body of the post,* without a header, without a section break, without any visual signal that it is coming. The reader gets the pitch *inside* a sentence that was already doing story work. See the 07-05 opener rewrite in §5 angle #2. Any post that arrives with any *"Rōmy"* section header — of any wording — is bounced.

- **"A small assignment, with love ♡" — 5 verbatim instances in 12 days.** Retire the entire *assignment* section for the next four posts. The post ends when the argument ends. Not when the reader has been given a homework packet. If the closing move of the essay is *"Open the address fields in your CRM. Look for donors clustered in five small towns…"* — that is a mailing, not a piece of writing. Working development directors do not need this blog to tell them how to run a query. They need this blog to give them a *sentence* worth taping to a monitor.

### Newly flagged this week — bench for two weeks

- **The `tag: "Field Notes"` frontmatter.** Retire immediately, or commit. If the Liturgy is a strategic sub-brand, name it, style it (different typography? different byline? different landing page?), and defend it. If it is not — pull the tag now, before it becomes a third-party category. Right now it is *the label the writer puts on a post to signal it is going to be templated.* That is a self-fulfilling category.

- **The *"in the plain [X] sense"* qualifier.** 18 instances in four posts this week. It is the writer's tell that she is about to say something she is not sure is true and wants to hedge. Retire the whole family — *"in the plainest sense," "in the plain honest sense," "in the plain field sense," "in any pretendable sense," "in the small quiet way,"* etc. If the sentence needs *in the plain sense* to be defensible, it is not defensible.

- **The word *small.*** 47 instances in 07-04 alone. It has become the writer's *emotional pigment.* Everything is small. Every gesture, every town, every letter, every kitchen, every note, every heart. When every thing is *small,* nothing is. Ration the word to *five uses per post,* and let the reader supply the size. The writing improves the instant this constraint is imposed.

- **The *"Be one of [X]. ♡"* closing imperative.** 6 verbatim instances in 14 days. Retire the *"Be one of"* construction *and* the *"Be [X]"* imperative closing entirely. If the essay's argument is that the reader should be a particular kind of person, the essay should have earned that by the closing — not commanded it. See 07-05's closing: *"The mail is already open. Rowan is holding it up."* No imperative. No *"Be one of"*. The reader knows what to do.

- **The dated-anniversary cold-open — *"On the second Sunday in July, at four-forty in the afternoon…"* / *"On the Fourth of July, at ten-forty in the morning…"* / *"On the second Thursday in July, at four-fourteen in the afternoon…"* — 3 of 4 posts.** The Tuesday-morning tic was retired; the calendar-anniversary tic replaced it. Both are the same *"a specific unremarkable moment in time"* device. The specific hour is now doing no work; it is signaling *this is a Rōmy post* the same way *"A small honest note about Rōmy"* did. Retire the hour-and-minute stamp for the next four posts. If the moment matters, describe it. If it does not, don't put a clock in the room.
